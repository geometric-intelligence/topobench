SheafTSP: Sheaf-Topological Signal Processing

Track 2 (TNN) submission — Topological Deep Learning Challenge 2026, TopoBench integration. This page explains the approach and the design principles behind it. Flow diagrams: architecturediagram.html. Full experiment log with per-study commands and verdicts: docs/BATTLE_PLAN.md in the project repository.

1. Main Contribution

SheafTSP is a spectral convolutional network whose diffusion operator is a learned, weighted Sheaf Laplacian, replacing the fixed graph or Hodge Laplacian. To be precise, the model builds a Hansen–Ghrist [2] graph sheaf over the 1-cell adjacency structure of a lifted cell complex — stalks and learned transports live on edge pairs; 2-cells contribute the adjacency pattern and the exact counting feature (Sec. 3.6). A full cellular sheaf with restriction maps up the coboundary (1-cells into 2-cells) is the natural extension and is future work. Each layer (i) learns orthogonal restriction maps between stalks via a Cayley-parameterized MLP, (ii) assembles the degree-normalized Sheaf Laplacian, (iii) lifts the channel signal into the stalk fibers, and (iv) applies a polynomial spectral filter. Alongside this diffusion machinery, the node embedding carries a deliberately unnormalized additive term derived from rank-2 structure. The central engineering lesson of this project (Sec. 4) is that community structure and substructure counting want opposite treatments of normalization — so the architecture keeps an operator-normalized diffusion signal and a strictly linear count term side by side in one embedding, protected from each other.

2. Core Hypotheses

2.1 Smoothness as transport-consistency

The standard graph Laplacian penalizes all neighbor differences — an implicit homophily assumption that oversmooths and fails on heterophilic graphs. The sheaf Laplacian's zero eigenspace consists of global sections: signals consistent with the learned restriction maps. Under this operator, smoothness requires only that each node's value agree with its neighbors' values after transport along the learned edge maps; the features themselves may differ arbitrarily [2][3]. This is the mechanism by which a sheaf model can handle low-homophily community structure that defeats similarity-based diffusion.

Measured evidence. Reading the learned $SO(2)$ transport angles out of trained checkpoints: on a mid-homophily benchmark setting the first layer learns mild rotations (mean $|\theta| = 9.9^\circ$, 68% of edges above $5^\circ$); on a low-homophily setting it learns near-antipodal maps (mean $|\theta| = 165.8^\circ$, 100% of edges non-trivial) — the transports discover that heterophilic neighbors are anti-correlated and flip them into consistency. In the magnetic-Laplacian view (Sec. 3.1) the model selects phases near $\pi$ (signed regime) under heterophily and near $0$ (standard diffusion) under homophily, per edge, from data.

2.2 Communities live in the bottom of the sheaf spectrum

Transport-consistent partitions of a graph appear as near-harmonic eigensections of $L_{\mathcal F}$ — a sheaf-native generalization of spectral clustering [1][2]. Consequently the community-detection signal is carried by the bottom of the spectrum, and the right filter shape is long-range and low-pass. The stability of the bottom spectrum under resampling [1] also makes it the transferable part (next hypothesis).

2.3 Spectral stability in the inductive setting

The challenge is inductive: train and test graphs are drawn from the same generative family at different sizes. We state precisely what is and is not guaranteed. Guaranteed: the normalized operator satisfies $\sigma(\hat L_{\mathcal F}) \subseteq [0,2]$ per input (verified numerically; the bound follows from the orthogonal block structure and kernel-weighted degrees), so the polynomial filters are numerically stable on every graph size. Not claimed: formal geometric transferability. The convergence theory of [1] — discrete sheaf Laplacians approaching a continuous connection Laplacian — is stated for sheaves whose stalks sit on sampled points (0-cells); SheafTSP's sheaf lives on the 1-cells of the lifted complex, and the corresponding convergence statements (Hodge-type limits for edge operators) are open. The design draws on [1] for motivation; cross-size generalization is an empirical claim in this submission, supported by the measured inductive results (Sec. 5) and consistent with the size-independent construction (amortized transports, bounded filters, near-identity initialization).

2.4 Expressivity position

Message-passing GNNs provably cannot count triangles: no MPNN or 2-IGN can count any connected induced substructure on three or more nodes [6] — the result that motivates this benchmark's counting task and the field's move to lifted (simplicial/cellular) message passing. SheafTSP makes no expressivity claim for its diffusion backbone; the design axis is different. The lifted complex supplies the structural information explicitly (as it does for every topological model in the challenge), and the architecture is engineered so that this information survives to the prediction — the property most architectures lose to normalization and mixing (Sec. 4, P1–P2). Counting accuracy then measures information preservation, and the grid results (Sec. 5) reflect that distinction.

3. Architecture

Equation tags (Eq. 8, 10, 15) cite Tandon et al. [1], whose architecture is dubbed "HilbNets": the kernel-weighted sheaf Laplacian (their Eq. 8, Defs. 3–4), the polynomial sheaf filter (their Eq. 10), and the transport regularizer added to the task loss (their Eq. 15, App. D; we use their $\lambda = 0.01$). SheafTSP's deltas from the framework are noted per equation below.

3.1 Learning the restriction maps

$$ S_{uv} = \mathrm{skew}\!\big(\mathrm{MLP}([x_u \| x_v]) - \mathrm{MLP}([x_v \| x_u])\big), \qquad R_e = (I - S)(I + S)^{-1} \in SO(d) $$
vs. NSD (Bodnar et al., 2022) [3]: NSD learns general or diagonal restriction maps with no orthogonality guarantee, so the induced Laplacian can be poorly conditioned. vs. BuNN (Bamberger et al., 2024) [4]: BuNN also uses orthogonal maps but within a flat-bundle heat-kernel scheme; SheafTSP uses the Cayley transform inside a per-edge learned map feeding a spectral filter. The MLP amortizes transports across graphs — required for the inductive setting, where per-edge transport parameters (as in [2]) are unavailable. The generator is antisymmetrized over the edge direction, so $R_{vu} = R_{uv}^{-1}$ holds by construction and the model is invariant to node relabeling. Precision notes: the Cayley image is the dense subset of $SO(d)$ excluding rotations with a $-1$ eigenvalue, and the bandwidth is detached inside the transport regularizer so alignment gradients reach the transports only.

At the default $d = 2$ the structure group $SO(2)$ is abelian, and the operator is real-conjugate to a magnetic Laplacian with learned, feature-dependent phases [7] — placing the default model in the U(1)-connection family, with amortized learned phases and the sheaf framing supplying the generalization to non-abelian $d \geq 3$.

Parameterization test: the antipodal exclusion is a real topological property of the Cayley map (reaching angle $\theta$ requires generator norm $\tan(\theta/2)$, divergent at $\pi$), and it was benchmarked against the surjective alternative. An exponential-map variant (verified to reach $-I$ exactly with bounded generators) scored 0.3172 vs Cayley's 0.3284 on the low-homophily setting — where the obstruction would bind — and 0.4075 vs 0.4151 at mid homophily. The learned optimum sits near $166^\circ$, reachable under Cayley with generator norms $\approx 8$ and stable convergence; the exclusion is measurably non-binding, and Cayley remains the default with the exponential map available in the codebase.

3.2 Weighted Sheaf Laplacian with learnable kernel (Eq. 8 of [1])

$$ k_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\rho_{ij}^2}{4t}\right), \qquad \rho_{ij}^2 = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert^2 \;\text{ or }\; \lVert s_i - R_e\, s_j \rVert^2, \qquad \hat L_{\mathcal F} = D^{-1/2}\, \delta^{\top} K \delta\, D^{-1/2} $$
vs. GCN / Hodge-Laplacian TNNs: those use fixed, data-independent operators. Here the coboundary $\delta$ carries the learned $R_e$ and $K$ is a Gaussian kernel on the distance $\rho_{ij}$ with learnable bandwidth $t$. The second distance form is the sheaf-native one and the submission default: it weights an edge by the inconsistency of the two endpoints under the learned transport. A raw-similarity kernel quietly reintroduces the homophily bias that hypothesis Sec. 2.1 exists to remove; measured under exact counting (Sec. 3.6) the transport form also improved the counting task. Symmetric normalization bounds the spectrum in $[0,2]$, keeping filter powers stable.

3.3 Lifting channels into the stalk fibers ([1] Prop. 1)

$$ s = \mathrm{Lift}(x): \;\; \mathbb{R}^{N \times C} \to \mathbb{R}^{Nd \times C} $$
vs. NSD's Kronecker broadcast [3]: a learned linear map gives each of the $d$ stalk coordinates distinct channel content — the stalk is treated as a genuine signal fiber, so the transports act on non-degenerate vectors.

3.4 Spectral filter (Eq. 10 of [1])

$$ y = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \hat L_{\mathcal F}^{\,k}\, s\, W_k \quad\text{(matrix coefficients, } W_0 = I \text{ init)} \qquad\text{or}\qquad y = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k\, P^k s,\;\; P = I - \tfrac{1}{2}\hat L_{\mathcal F} $$
vs. one-hop message passing (GIN, GCN): GIN and GCN mix one hop per layer; each SheafTSP layer applies a $K$-hop sheaf-aware filter. The second (scalar-coefficient) form is the low-pass long-range realization of hypothesis Sec. 2.2 and the submission default ($K = 10$): $P$ is the sheaf lazy walk with spectrum in $[0,1]$, so large $K$ is numerically stable, and initializing $w_k = \alpha(1-\alpha)^k$ makes the layer personalized-PageRank diffusion over the sheaf at epoch 0. Raw monomial powers of $\hat L_{\mathcal F}$ (spectrum $[0,2]$) explode at large $K$ — depth requires either this operator change or a Chebyshev basis.

3.5 Transport-alignment regularizer (Eq. 15 of [1])

$$ \mathcal L = \mathcal L_{\text{task}} + \lambda \sum_{\ell} \mathrm{tr}\!\left(s_\ell^{\top} L_{\mathcal F}^{(\ell)} s_\ell\right), \qquad \lambda = 0.01 $$
vs. unregularized sheaf learning (NSD) [3]: the signal is detached, so gradients flow only into the transports $R$ and bandwidth $t$ — the learned geometry is pulled toward configurations where the observed signal is smooth in the sheaf sense, without flattening the signal itself. A theoretical concern with alignment pressure is geometric trivialization (transports collapsing toward global sections and erasing the non-trivial holonomy that distinguishes cyclic structure). The measured transport distributions rule this out at $\lambda = 0.01$: learned angles span the group with large spread (Sec. 2.1; low-homophily settings: mean $|\theta| = 165.8^\circ$, $\sigma = 167^\circ$), and the $\lambda = 0$ ablation was the worst-performing configuration tested — the regularizer is load-bearing and the geometry it produces is far from trivial.

3.6 The rank-2 count term

$$ t_v = |B_1|\,|B_2|\,\mathbf 1, \qquad x_0 \mathrel{+}= W_{\text{tri}}\, t_v, \qquad \textstyle\sum_{v \in G} t_v = 6 \cdot \#\text{triangles}(G) $$
The count signal is derived inside the model from the lifted complex's own incidence matrices — the same $B_1, B_2$ every lifted architecture in the challenge consumes. Under the clique lifting (Sec. 3.7) this degree signal is exact (each triangle at a node contributes 2; verified against direct enumeration), so the graph total is exactly linear in the count and the sum-pooled regression head recovers the answer by learning a single scale factor. The counting signal and the diffusion operator draw on one canonical triangle set. $W_{\text{tri}}$ is zero-initialized with one warm channel, so tasks that do not benefit keep it switched off. Two properties of the count term's route to the prediction are worth stating: every operation from $t_v$ to the prediction is linear with no normalization, and the pipeline's incidence matrices are unsigned ($B_1 \in \{0,1\}^{N_0\times N_1}$, column sums $=2$), so global sum-pooling aggregates the boundary-projected diffusion signal — the cancellation $\mathbf 1^\top B_1 = \mathbf 0^\top$ that holds for the signed boundary operator does not arise.

Provenance note: under a cycle-basis lifting the same formula undercounts on dense graphs (measured: 59 basis cells for 139 true triangles), which is one of the two reasons the clique lifting is the default (Sec. 3.7). Diagnostic details: docs/BATTLE_PLAN.md in the project repository.

On "the preprocessing does the counting": every topological model in this challenge consumes precomputed higher-order structure — the counting leader's clique lifting hands it every triangle as an explicit 2-cell, and the benchmark exists to test whether architectures exploit such structure. The measured differentiator is what happens in transit: most architectures destroy cardinality information between the structure and the prediction (P2 — a BatchNorm difference alone separates two otherwise similar models by 6× on this task). SheafTSP's contribution is an end-to-end route engineered to preserve it, and the counting margin in Sec. 5 is the measurement of that engineering.

3.7 Permutation-invariant lifting

The complex is lifted by attaching all 3-cliques of the raw graph as 2-cells. The triangle set of a graph is canonical, so the lifted complex — and every connectivity matrix derived from it — is invariant under node relabeling. Cycle-basis liftings lack this property: the basis returned by spanning-tree traversal depends on node ordering, and we measured the same 20-node graph receiving 11 vs 10 2-cells with only 8 shared under a random relabeling. With the clique lifting the measured 2-cell sets are identical under relabeling and complete (14/14 true triangles). Combined with the orientation-equivariant transports (Sec. 3.1), the full pipeline is invariant to node relabeling.

Regime note: on sparse graphs (average degree ≈ 2) triangles are almost surely edge-disjoint, so a cycle basis coincides with the full triangle set (20/20 sampled graphs identical); on dense graphs (average degree ≈ 4.5) it does not (2/20), which is where the canonical choice carries its weight.

4. Design principles

P1 — Two normalization regimes in one embedding. The two challenge tasks are antagonistic under a single operator. Community detection wants normalized, well-conditioned, long-range low-pass diffusion (Sec. 2.2). Counting wants strict sum-linearity: every operation between the counted cells and the prediction must be an unnormalized sum. The evidence is stark across the field [5]: the community-detection leader is a pure normalized diffusion model and is the worst leader at counting; the counting leader is pure unnormalized summation and mediocre at communities; and two architectures with nearly identical message schemes differ 6× in counting error purely because one inserts BatchNorm. In SheafTSP the two regimes coexist as additive terms of one node embedding: the sheaf-diffused signal (Sec. 3.4) is operator-normalized, and the exact count term (Sec. 3.6) reaches the prediction through strictly linear, unnormalized operations.
P2 — Normalization destroys counts. LayerNorm, BatchNorm, degree averaging, or mean pooling anywhere between a cardinality signal and the prediction removes exactly the magnitude information that carries the count. We found this independently (a post-hoc LayerNorm residual silently nullified the entire count term) before finding the same signature in the field's results [5]. Corollary: audit the whole route from structure to prediction, including inherited wrapper and readout code.
P3 — Regularization belongs on the learned geometry. Dropout on the diffused signal corrupts the count term and fights the regression tasks: measured in isolation it degrades counting by three orders of magnitude, and although combining it with PPR diffusion partially masks the damage, the submission excludes signal dropout entirely. Dropout on the restriction-map learner's input regularizes the learned transports — where overfitting actually lives — and leaves the signal clean. Zero-initialized terms additionally need a warm-start channel to survive noisy training: a term that starts at exactly zero never matures before early stopping fires.
P4 — Weight edges by transport-consistency. A Gaussian kernel on raw feature distance contradicts hypothesis Sec. 2.1 inside the very operator built to embody it. The consistency form $\lVert s_i - R_e s_j\rVert^2$ makes the kernel and the transports tell the same story. (Related, from [2]: quadratic penalties on dissimilar pairs let cross-community edges dominate transport gradients; bounded, saturating forms behave better.)
P5 — Audit structural signals against ground truth. A signal that is "exact by construction" is exact only if its construction matches the definition being predicted. Our count channel silently inherited a cycle lifting's basis semantics and undercounted dense graphs 2–3×; the tell was behavioral (training shrank the channel's weight). The remedy in the submitted model is structural: a canonical all-triangles lifting under which the incidence-derived signal is provably exact (Sec. 3.6–3.7). Verify derived structure against a direct computation at least once.
P6 — Bandwidth is a function of scale (future work). Consistency theory [2] requires the kernel bandwidth to shrink with sample count ($t \propto n^{-1/(m+2)}$); a size-fixed bandwidth is inconsistent across the different graph sizes of an inductive family. The submission learns a single scalar $t$; size-conditioned bandwidth (e.g., initialized from the standard deviation of pairwise feature distances per graph) is a documented, unimplemented refinement.

At time of writing, SheafTSP posts the lowest triangle-counting error among published submissions (3.1× under the next-lowest) and the second-highest community-detection accuracy. The counting result follows from Sec. 3.6: with an exact structural signal and a sum-linear route to the prediction, the task reduces to learning one scale factor, and the residual error measures optimization variance; architectural capacity is no longer the limiting factor.

Per-setting 3-seed means for this submission: CD 0.322–0.333 (low homophily), 0.406–0.486 (mid), 0.614–0.719 (high); TRI 0.0008–0.026. TRI seed-level outliers occur (a zero-initialized count channel occasionally matures late under early stopping; 5 of 36 runs) and the reported means include them. Full per-run values are in results.json.

Methodological note: the counting design traces to a diagnosis — a trained model kept shrinking the weight of a count channel believed to be exact, which exposed the cycle-basis undercount (Sec. 3.6, P5) and led to the canonical lifting and the endogenous signal the submission ships.
